Phantom Touch

Designing a Multimodal Wearable Interface for VR

Date
November 2026
Type
Independent XR and physical-computing project
Status
Phase 1 complete; no participant results reported

Current stage

Phase 1 is complete. The ERM glove hardware, keyboard application and end-to-end integration are in place. Work now moves to Phase 2, multi-finger LRA feedback. Participant evaluation remains to be completed.

The vision: feel virtual surfaces with free fingerpads

Phantom Touch is an independent research project exploring how virtual surface properties can become tactile feedback through a wearable interface. The central goal is to sample UV-indexed height and roughness maps along a finger's contact path and render their features through carefully characterized linear resonant actuators (LRAs). Meta Quest 3 supplies optical hand tracking; actuators mounted on the nails or backs of the fingers aim to leave the fingerpads available for touching physical objects.

Higher-fidelity feedback is a development goal to be measured through temporal response and perceptual discrimination. The initial ERM hardware and keyboard scene establish the communication path, finger addressing and contact-event logic needed for that investigation. Typing is a test application within the larger interface project.

A central design decision: keep the fingerpads free

Feel the virtual. Stay in touch with the physical.

The interface is designed to leave the fingers' touch surfaces exposed, allowing direct skin contact with physical objects while adding feedback from virtual interactions. Preserving access to the physical world is a core requirement for the wearable, alongside the quality of its virtual feedback.

The open-finger construction places actuators on the nails or backs of the fingers, routes wiring along the back of the hand and locates the main electronics at the wrist. The fingerpads remain uncovered. Intended everyday interactions include picking up a cup, handling small objects and using a physical keyboard without removing the wearable. These are design targets to validate, not demonstrated capabilities.

This choice introduces a research tradeoff: feedback delivered away from the fingerpad may feel different from direct fingertip stimulation. Mounting, wiring and glove material may also affect dexterity or optical tracking. Evaluation will therefore examine both virtual texture perception and physical interaction, comparing simple object-handling tasks with and without the wearable and recording completion time, handling errors, comfort and tracking interruptions.

The same design principle will guide later thermal and muscle-feedback modules: preserve exposed fingerpads and assess whether each addition interferes with physical interaction. Module placement and the location at which users perceive feedback remain open design questions.

Phase 1: an inexpensive hardware-to-VR integration test

Wearable hardware

Two glove-mounted assemblies, each with an ESP32-S3, a TCA9548A multiplexer, five DRV2605L drivers and five coin ERM motors. Electronics sit at the wrist; flexible wiring runs along the back of the fingers.

Virtual keyboard

A standalone Quest 3 application shows tracked hands, a keyboard and a text panel. Accepted presses append characters and trigger the matching finger's actuator. Backspace and space support basic correction and phrase entry.

Phase 1 integration architecture

The keyboard as an integration test

A press is accepted once when a fingertip enters a key's activation region. The finger must withdraw before the key can fire again. Tracking loss cancels pending contact; reacquisition must not generate a keypress automatically. The same acceptance event updates the text panel, visual key highlight and haptic command.

The first version uses large keys, one active press at a time and short phrases. It does not assume that camera-based hand tracking can reliably distinguish rapid ten-finger typing. An offline motor sequence verified finger-to-actuator mapping before headset integration.

Prior work and literature review

A focused review connects three strands of research: sensing finger input, delivering wearable feedback, and synthesizing surface textures. These papers inform the design and help define what still needs to be tested in this implementation. This is a selected review, not a systematic survey.

Finger input for VR

Meier et al. — TapID: Rapid Touch Interaction in Virtual Reality using Wearable Sensing (IEEE VR, 2021) combines wrist inertial sensing with tracked hand poses to detect surface taps and identify the tapping finger, including for keyboard input.

Design implication: detecting a press and confirming it are separate problems. Phantom Touch initially uses optical hand tracking; adding a vibration does not by itself improve touch detection.

Wireless finger haptics

Preechayasomboon & Rombokas — Haplets (Frontiers in Virtual Reality, 2021) presents finger-worn wireless LRA devices integrated with Quest hand tracking, including texture demonstrations.

Design implication: controller-free finger haptics and LRA texture feedback already have precedent. Phantom Touch begins with inexpensive ERMs to establish an accessible implementation and test event delivery before investigating texture rendering.

Feedback through the fingernail

Tran et al. — Augmenting On-Body Touch Input with Tactile Feedback Through Fingernail Haptics (CHI, 2023) studies tactile augmentation, click sensations and expressive effects for on-body input.

Design implication: fingernail actuation is a supported design direction, but findings for on-body touch cannot establish benefits for an ungrounded virtual keyboard. Typing performance and comfort require their own evaluation.

Texture maps as haptic inputs

Chan, Tymms & Colonnese — Hasti: Haptic and Audio Synthesis for Texture Interactions (IEEE World Haptics, 2021) synthesizes vibration and sound from contact trajectories, displacement maps and roughness maps using a micro-contact model.

Design implication: rendering haptics from graphics texture maps is established prior work. The future contribution must concern how a constrained wearable renders those maps and how users perceive the result.

Movement-dependent texture perception

Friesen & Vardar — Perceived Realism of Virtual Textures Rendered by a Vibrotactile Wearable Ring Display (IEEE Transactions on Haptics, 2024) investigates virtual texture realism using a wearable ring and vibration mappings linked to exploration speed.

Design implication: texture feedback should be evaluated across finger speeds and spatial scales. A convincing demonstration at one speed does not establish consistent texture perception.

Positioning this interface

Hasti's evaluation apparatus used a pose-tracked mesh glove with an actuator against the index fingertip. Its rendering framework also explicitly accommodates other tracking inputs, including vision-based tracking. Phantom Touch proposes a different implementation configuration: headset-based Quest hand tracking and dorsal or nail-mounted actuation with exposed fingerpads. This changes the tracking and tactile coupling conditions that must be evaluated; it does not by itself establish a new rendering method.

Haplets already combines Quest tracking, finger-worn LRAs and free fingerpads. The research opportunity therefore lies in evaluating map-driven rendering in this configuration: how tracking uncertainty, mounting location and actuator response affect which surface features remain perceptible, and whether an adaptive renderer improves consistency.

Phase 1 validation: establish a reliable feedback link

The keyboard gives each contact event an observable result: a character on screen and a pulse on the corresponding finger. Offline motor sequences checked channel mapping; headset tests checked correct routing, unwanted repeats, missed commands, release behavior and recovery after tracking loss. These are integration checks, not evidence that texture rendering works or that typing performance improves.

I plan to measure physical vibration onset and variability, communication reliability, comfort, and the effect of dorsal wiring on hand tracking. Network acknowledgments alone do not measure when the motor begins vibrating. The texture study below is the main planned perceptual evaluation; a typing-performance study was not required for this first phase.

Core research direction: UV and roughness maps with LRAs

Proposed research question

Can a renderer that accounts for finger speed, tracking uncertainty and measured LRA response preserve distinguishable surface features more consistently than direct map-to-vibration playback on a low-cost, hand-tracked glove?

The gap this project aims to address is practical and perceptual: which spatial features can this constrained system reliably convey, and how should it simplify a texture when the requested events exceed its measured capabilities? The selected literature motivates this question but does not establish that it is an unstudied gap. This remains a research hypothesis to refine through further review and experiments.

Planned rendering pipeline

  1. Locate contact: project the tracked fingertip onto the virtual surface and obtain its UV coordinates. UVs locate samples on the object; they are not themselves a texture property.
  2. Read the maps: sample a height or boundary map for ridges and transitions, and a separate haptic roughness map for local variation. A visual material's roughness map may provide an authoring starting point, but it is not a calibrated measure of tactile roughness.
  3. Account for motion: use physical distance traveled and finger speed so that events follow surface features rather than a fixed timer. Account for UV stretching so equal texture intervals do not silently imply equal distances on every mesh.
  4. Respect the hardware: convert sampled features into amplitude envelopes and transient events appropriate for the selected LRA and driver. Test whether filtering or merging events improves perception when features are too close together. LRAs operate around resonance; replacing ERMs does not create arbitrary-frequency or force feedback.
  5. Handle uncertain tracking: suppress contact events during tracking loss and require stable contact before resuming. Measure whether this reduces unwanted sensations without hiding meaningful features.

How the question will be evaluated

The planned texture pilot will compare direct map playback against the proposed constrained renderer on the same LRA hardware, with no-haptics trials as a reference. Participants will distinguish coarse ridge spacing or identify a rougher patch while exploring at controlled slow and fast speeds. Geometry, visual appearance and audio will be held constant within comparisons; actuator intensity will be calibrated and differences in total stimulation recorded to avoid interpreting a stronger cue as better texture rendering.

Outcomes will include identification accuracy, roughness judgments, consistency across speeds, perceived continuity and comfort. Technical measurements will document physical vibration onset, tracking dropouts and missed or merged events. Initial tests will use one finger and a few simple maps before expanding to multiple fingers. The intended output is an operating-range characterization and tested rendering guidelines, not a claim to reproduce realistic materials.

Project phases

  • Phase 1 (complete): ERM keypress confirmation and integration checks.
  • Phase 2: multi-finger LRA characterization, UV-map sampling and a small comparative texture study, with an openly documented account of what works, what fails and why.
  • Phase 3: exploring electrical muscle stimulation to simulate recoil at the wrist for FPS shooters.

The texture renderer and its evaluation are planned work, not results of the current prototype.

An extensible interface for richer VR sensations

The longer-term vision remains open to additional feedback modalities and sensing capabilities. A shared interaction layer would describe contact, sliding, impact and material properties; separate modules would translate these into the feedback each device can actually deliver. Each extension would need its own hardware characterization and perceptual evaluation.

Thermal feedback

A future module could investigate warm and cool object cues alongside vibration. Its research question would concern whether thermal cues improve material recognition or presence, accounting for their slower response. Any skin-contact implementation would require temperature sensing, independent limits and appropriate supervised evaluation.

Impact and movement cues

Phase 3 explores coordinated tactile and kinesthetic feedback for virtual impacts, focusing on muscle stimulation to simulate recoil at the wrist in FPS shooters. Lopes et al. — Impacto (UIST, 2015) provides precedent for combining a mechanical skin tap with electrical muscle stimulation to convey impact. EMS would be a separate, specialist-supervised research effort using purpose-built stimulation equipment, rather than an extension of the motor-driver circuit.

Immersive action games such as Half-Life: Alyx illustrate a possible application context for synchronized impact cues. This is an application ambition: no game integration, compatibility or recoil reproduction has been demonstrated. An initial impact experiment would use a controlled custom scene. Thermal feedback remains an exploratory direction beyond the planned phases.

Scope and current status

Phase 1 delivered a documented ERM assembly and a minimal keyboard integration demonstration. The main research direction is higher-fidelity map-driven tactile feedback; LRA selection, texture rendering and participant evaluation remain planned. The first texture pilot will counterbalance conditions, provide practice and report uncertainty; sample size and session duration will follow feasibility testing and applicable research review.

Vibration alone cannot reproduce surface resistance, key travel or a physical recoil force. Fingerpads are intended to remain exposed, but tracking quality and the perceptual consequences of dorsal mounting must be measured. Heart-rate sensing and wrist displays are optional supporting features outside the core texture study.
